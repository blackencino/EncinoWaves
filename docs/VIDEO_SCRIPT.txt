ENCINO WAVES — FIVE-MINUTE PRESENTATION DRAFT
Christopher J. Horvath

This is a narration and shot plan for review. It makes no claim about particular
films, adoption counts, production savings, or award status. Add specific credited
production examples only where you want them and can substantiate them.
The generated movie has captions and no recorded narration or music.

0:00–0:20 | OPENING
[Open ocean. Let the image hold before speaking.]
An ocean has a character. It can feel restless, quiet, powerful, or distant.
For a visual effects artist, that character has to serve a shot. Encino Waves
is a way to shape it with a small set of controls tied to the physical world.

0:20–0:45 | THE STARTING POINT
[Matched earlier-model / Encino comparison.]
This work builds on the ocean method introduced to computer graphics by
Jerry Tessendorf. That method gave artists an extraordinary starting point.
But getting a particular sea often meant balancing additional layers and
adjustments by eye. My work brings measured descriptions of real oceans
into that process, so the basic controls do more of the work.

0:45–1:30 | WIND SPEED
[5, 17, then 30 metres per second. Same camera and lighting.]
Start with wind speed. A light wind gives us a much quieter surface.
Increase the wind and the waves grow. Increase it further and the water
becomes more energetic. We are changing one physical description of the
scene, rather than independently sculpting every size of wave.
[Leave room for the motion to be seen.]

1:30–2:15 | FETCH
[20, 300, then 1,250 kilometres.]
Wind speed is only part of the story. The wind also needs room to build waves.
That distance is called fetch. A wind blowing across a short stretch of water
produces a different sea from the same wind blowing across a much longer
stretch. This gives an artist a useful way to change the character of the
water while keeping the controls connected to a physical idea.

2:15–2:35 | DIRECTIONAL SPREADING
[Matched comparison, with empirical spreading selected.]
Real waves do not all travel in exactly the same direction. Different sizes
of wave spread differently. Bringing that behavior into the model changes
the organization of the surface and the way its shapes move through a shot.

2:35–3:15 | SWELL
[Swell zero, then one.]
A local wind can make a choppy, irregular sea. Waves travelling from a distant
weather system can arrive as long, parallel swells. The swell control provides
a direct way to move between those appearances. The artist can explore that
range without managing a collection of hidden adjustments.

3:15–3:45 | DEPTH
[150 metres, then 5 metres.]
Depth matters too. The same wind over shallow water produces a different mix
of waves. Here the model responds to the depth itself. This is a description
of the open surface; breaking surf and interaction with a shoreline remain
separate parts of a production workflow.

3:45–4:25 | RANGE
[Light wind, then storm. Give each image time.]
These are different conditions produced by the same model: a light breeze
and a strong storm. The point is the range an artist can reach through
controls that are understandable and repeatable.

4:25–4:40 | THE TOOL
[Show the controls briefly in an optional separately recorded viewer insert.]
Wind speed. Fetch. Directional spreading. Swell. Depth. Each has a clear role.
The artist can make a choice, see the result, and return to exactly the same
state later.

4:40–5:00 | CLOSE
[Hold on the ocean.]
The aim of Encino Waves is to make physically grounded oceans practical to
art-direct. The research is in the model. The value to the artist is in the
image, and in how directly they can reach the ocean a shot needs.
