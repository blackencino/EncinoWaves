ENCINO WAVES — FIVE-MINUTE CONTINUOUS PRESENTATION
Christopher J. Horvath

Narration draft for review. The generated movie has captions and no recorded
narration or music. Leave pauses to watch the water. Only one physical control
changes at a time. Camera moves occur while the ocean controls hold still.
The patch stays one kilometre across, with the same seed, lighting and foam
history throughout. This demonstration edits the ocean's prescribed conditions;
it does not represent the time real weather takes to transform a sea.

0:00–0:22 | DEEP OCEAN
[Deep water. Hold the horizon, then ease to the raised overview.]
An ocean has a character. It can feel restless, quiet, powerful, or threatening.
For a visual effects artist, that character has to serve the shot. Encino Waves
gives the artist a small set of controls tied to the physical world.

0:22–0:42 | THE STARTING POINT
[The earlier model fades onto the left half. Camera and lighting stay fixed.
The comparison uses Pierson–Moskowitz with cosine-squared directional spreading.
Fade the comparison away at 0:39, returning to the continuously running ocean.]
This builds on the ocean method introduced to computer graphics by Jerry
Tessendorf. Here is an earlier description beside Encino Waves. Real seas have
different sizes of waves travelling in different directions. Bringing measured
descriptions of that behavior into the model changes how the water moves.

0:42–1:00 | WIND SPEED
[Wind eases from 17 to 5 m/s over twelve seconds; hold six seconds.]
First, ease the wind. Nothing else changes. The surface becomes quieter.
The artist does not have to sculpt every size of wave separately.

1:00–1:22 | FETCH, THEN SWELL
[Fetch 300 to 20 km, then swell 0.35 to −0.6. Separate edits.]
Wind also needs room to build waves. That distance is called fetch.
Here it has only a short stretch of water to work with.
Now reduce the swell, spreading the directions of travel.

1:22–2:10 | CHAOS
[Raise only wind, from 5 to 32 m/s. Hold, then move across the crests.]
Keep those conditions and bring in a strong wind.
This is one thing a director might ask for: chaos. Restless, confused water.
There is a lot of energy, but little of the long, organized rhythm of a swell.
[Let the ocean play through the camera move.]

2:10–2:34 | FETCH
[Wind stays at 32 m/s. Fetch grows from 20 to 1,250 km, then holds.]
The same strong wind can give us a very different sea.
Give it more distance to build waves. Change only the fetch.
The water grows in scale while the swell setting stays where it was.

2:34–3:34 | LAWFUL EVIL
[Raise only swell, −0.6 to 1.2. Hold the resulting sea.
At 3:04, ease to Sunset: height 92.5 m, pitch −15.4°, heading −487.6°.]
Now organize those waves into long trains.
The wind is just as strong, but the character is different: lawful evil.
Powerful and ordered. A different direction for the same shot.
An artist can explore these choices through the conditions themselves,
without compensating with a collection of unrelated adjustments.
[Leave substantial room for the sunset view and the moving waves.]

3:34–4:22 | SHALLOW CHOP
[Lower swell to −0.7 first. Then lower depth from 1,000 to 1.5 m.
Wind and fetch stay fixed. Hold the shallow result for twelve seconds.]
Spread the directions again. Now change the depth.
Very shallow water changes the mix of waves that can live on the surface.
With negative swell, this becomes chop. We have reached another distinct
character by changing one physical description at a time.

4:22–4:54 | RETURN TO DEEP WATER
[Restore depth first. Only after depth reaches 1,000 m, restore swell to 1.2.]
Return to deep water, then bring back the organized swell.
The waves keep moving through every adjustment. Foam from the previous sea
lingers and fades. There are no cuts hiding the changes.

4:54–5:00 | CLOSE
[Hold the sunset sea.]
Physically grounded oceans, shaped for the shot.
